# Source Bible · The Lost World

## Source range

This analysis covers all sixteen chapter bodies in the exact Project Gutenberg
eBook #139 bytes recorded in `../source/SOURCE.md`. It does not use a synopsis,
film imagery, a later adaptation, or the table of contents as a substitute for
the novel. `analysis/_coverage.md` preserves the source boundaries and the
16-source / 16-success / 0-failure count.

The text is treated as evidence written in 1912, not as a scientific account of
dinosaurs or human evolution. Species identifications spoken by characters can
remain uncertain even when the characters sound authoritative.

## World identity

The novel's durable identity is an expedition that must turn an incredible
claim into proof while crossing an environment that defeats direct access. The
plateau first appears as incomplete records—a sketchbook, damaged photographs,
a physical fragment and a survivor's account—then becomes a connected ecology
that has to be read through tracks, calls, feeding behavior, terrain and local
knowledge. Discovering the world is not enough: evidence must survive the route
home and withstand an audience prepared to reject it.

The strongest playable tension is therefore **wonder versus recoverability**.
Moving farther produces better knowledge and greater danger while making the
return path, supplies and proof more fragile. The text repeatedly rewards
observation, route memory and improvised protection before force.

## Evidence-backed world rules

Every row separates source fact from the adaptation question it creates.

| Rule cluster | Source evidence | Boundary | Consequence for concept selection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access is a chain of physical thresholds | A concealed tributary and forest tunnel lead to the plateau; the cliffs still require a chimney, a detached pinnacle and a felled-tree bridge (Chapters 8–9). | `immutable` | A concept must make approach, entry and exit materially distinct states. |
| Entry does not guarantee exit | Gomez drops the bridge after the expedition crosses; the known lower tunnel is blocked, while a different forked cave later provides a rope descent (Chapters 9, 15). | `immutable` | Route state must be able to change, and extraction knowledge must be earned. |
| Terrain is readable through natural signals | The brook reliably connects camp and lake; animal paths, calls, tracks, glades, canopy height and distant cave fires reveal location and risk (Chapters 10–12). | `immutable` | Navigation can rely on sound, silhouette and ecology rather than floating markers alone. |
| Observation changes available action | Bird-pecked fruit indicates safer food; three-toed tracks lead to iguanodons; asphalt marks are later recognized as ownership marks; a canopy view reveals the lake and caves (Chapters 10–11, 14). | `immutable` | Documentation must reveal a route, classification or safety decision, not only fill a collection. |
| Creatures have different perception and counterplay | Pterodactyls attack exposed intruders but trees impede their wings; one carnivore follows scent before switching to sight; the ape-people are described as keen-eyed/hearing but not scent trackers (Chapters 10, 12–13). | `adaptable` | Threats need legible, distinct detection states; exact ranges and timers are open. |
| Force is costly and sometimes ineffective | A gunshot carries for miles, fire repels a stalking carnivore, rifles fail to stop the huge nocturnal predators quickly, and elevated narrow stairs exclude them (Chapters 11, 15). | `immutable` | A firearm may buy time or redirect danger, but cannot make extermination the dominant loop. |
| Shelter is conditional | A thorn zareba and watch rotation reduce night risk, yet a tree-borne attack still overruns the camp; elevated caves resist the largest predators (Chapters 10–11, 13, 15). | `immutable` | Safety depends on the threat and can expire when the threat class changes. |

| Character classifications are fallible | Challenger and Summerlee disagree on species and repeatedly reserve judgment; some creatures remain unnamed or only approximately classified (Chapters 10–11, 15–16). | `immutable` | The interface should distinguish an observation from a confirmed identification. |

## Locked player identity validation

The `PRODUCT_BRIEF` locks the player as the expedition's forward scout. The
novel does not assign that formal title, so the identity is **adaptable**, built
from source-accessible work rather than invented authority.

| Accessible action / decision | Source support | Validation |
|---|---|---|
| Carry notebook, camera or portable specimen evidence | Malone reports and keeps records; the party uses photographs, maps, collections and a captured creature (Chapters 2, 4, 11–12, 16). | Supported. Evidence is central to the expedition's mandate. |
| Leave shelter to reconnoiter, then return with route knowledge | Malone climbs the gingko and later follows the brook to the lake and back (Chapters 11–12). | Supported, with explicit personal and team risk. |
| Read tracks, calls, feeding traces and terrain | The party uses footprints, bird-pecked fruit, water sound, paths, canopy views and cave fires (Chapters 10–12). | Supported as shared expedition practice, not supernatural tracking. |
| Choose exposure, cover, fire, noise or retreat | Trees shelter the party from pterodactyls; fire repels a carnivore; gunfire is withheld because it broadcasts position (Chapters 10–11). | Supported. Exact controls and meters remain open. |
| Use a limited defensive firearm | The expedition carries rifles; shots rescue companions or distract threats but do not reliably kill every creature (Chapters 10–15). | Supported, provided shooting is not the primary proof action. |
| Decode and verify an extraction route | The party compares an eighteen-mark chart to the caves, notices a missed fork, and tests the passage before leaving (Chapter 15). | Supported. Local knowledge must not be erased or reassigned to the scout. |

The identity does **not** inherit Challenger's scientific certainty, Roxton's
combat mastery, the Accala's local knowledge or Zambo's logistical labor. A
prototype may compress the ensemble's field tools into one controllable role,
but it must credit observations as observations and preserve the limits above.

## World and spatial model

The source supports a sequence of connected zones rather than an undifferentiated
open world:

1. **Amazon approach** — broad river to narrower tributary, rapids and portage,
   then a reed-screened entrance and dark forest tunnel (Chapters 7–8).
2. **Cliff foot and detached pinnacle** — fern plain, red basalt wall, Maple
   White camp/sign, blocked cave, bog and a gap bridged by a felled beech
   (Chapters 8–9).
3. **Fort Challenger edge camp** — thorn enclosure under a giant gingko, spring
   and brook, with the cliff and Zambo's lower camp maintaining a vertical link
   (Chapters 9–12).
4. **Iguanodon glade** — open grazing ground, track-rich approaches and evidence
   of predation and ownership (Chapters 10–11, 14).
5. **Pterodactyl rookery** — a wooded rim around a volcanic depression with blue
   clay, nests, foul air and little aerial room under the trees (Chapters 10,
   15–16).
6. **Forest and asphalt pool** — dense visibility, animal paths, audible water,
   volcanic heat and concealed human traps (Chapters 11–12).
7. **Central lake** — reeds, sandbanks, large aquatic silhouettes and a route to
   the opposite cliff (Chapters 11–12, 14–15).
8. **Accala cave escarpment** — narrow high stairs, dry passages, charcoal animal
   pictures, stores and eighteen visible openings (Chapters 14–15).
9. **Forked exit cave** — misleading cul-de-sac, missed dark branch, symbol-lined
   tunnel, moonlit rift and a final rope descent (Chapter 15).

The exact distances are not a prototype requirement. The `immutable` relation
is edge camp → brook/forest → lake → far caves, with the rookery and glade as
recognizable side spaces and the cliffs enclosing all of them.

## Threat classes and counter-evidence

1. **Aerial / territorial:** pterodactyls detect an exposed rookery intrusion,
   mob the party and inflict toxic wounds; dense trees restrict their wings
   (Chapters 10–11).
2. **Nocturnal terrestrial predator:** a large carnivorous form approaches by
   sound/scent, switches to visual pursuit, recoils from flame and is not
   reliably stopped by ordinary rifle fire (Chapters 11–12, 15).
3. **Terrain and route:** cliffs, chasms, bog, rapids, concealed stake pit,
   blocked tunnel and missed cave fork can strand or kill without an enemy
   encounter (Chapters 8–9, 12, 15).
4. **Human / social:** deception, revenge, secrecy, captivity, refusal of local
   aid and public disbelief can destroy access, proof or extraction (Chapters
   3, 5, 9, 13–16).

Counter-evidence matters: a rifle is powerful in the rescue of Chapter 13 but
ineffective against the huge predators in Chapter 15; a fortified camp deters
one night threat but is penetrated from the canopy in Chapter 13; photographs
motivate the expedition in Chapter 4 but do not settle public doubt in Chapter
16. No single tool is a universal answer.

## Evidence and proof lifecycle

```text
trace or testimony
  -> observe from a chosen position
  -> record as note / sketch / photograph / specimen
  -> interpret with an explicit confidence level
  -> protect the record while route conditions change
  -> extract the record
  -> submit it to an audience with its own proof threshold
```

Source examples include Maple White's notebook and damaged photograph, the
pterodactyl wing, Malone's chart and letters, Summerlee's collections, damaged
camera negatives, remaining photographs and the live pterodactyl (Chapters 4,
11–12, 15–16). A concept that awards full success at the moment of taking a
picture would omit the novel's final and most important reversal.

  a chasm (Chapters 4, 8–9).
- A felled beech spanning the gap, then the empty gap after the trunk falls
  away (Chapter 9).
- A thorn fort under an enormous gingko, with firelight below an arched dark
  canopy (Chapters 10–11).
- Three-toed tracks opening onto a family of iguanodons in a green glade
  (Chapter 10).
- A volcanic blue-clay rookery filled with leathery wings, cries and foul odor,
  seen from the cover of trees (Chapter 10).
- An oval forest basin sloping toward a green central lake, with gold sandbanks
  and a red inner escarpment punctured by cave mouths (Chapter 11).
- A black asphalt pool bubbling with heat inside dense, moonlit forest
  (Chapter 12).
- Distant cave fires across silver water (Chapter 12).
- High narrow cave stairs and smooth gray walls carrying charcoal animal
  pictures (Chapter 15).
- A dark tunnel ending in a circular plane of moonlight that resolves into the
  exit rift (Chapter 15).

Audio evidence includes running water as a navigation signal, the low breathing
and pad of a stalking predator, the shriek/growl pair of a kill, wing beats and
rookery cries, gunfire that carries across the plateau, and distant creature
calls over the lake (Chapters 10–12, 15–16).

These anchors come from the text. No modern film or game rendition supplies a
creature silhouette, palette, camera composition, score or sound effect.

## Adaptation boundaries

### `immutable`

- The plateau is physically isolated by basalt cliffs and contains a connected
  forest basin, central lake, rookery, glades and inhabited cave escarpment.
- Entry becomes blocked after arrival, and a different, knowledge-dependent
  route is required to extract.
- Observation and preserved proof—not killing—are the expedition's mandate.
- Threats expose different counters; fire, cover, height, quiet and firearms do
  not work equally against every danger.
- Evidence can be damaged, misclassified or rejected after it is collected.
- Local people possess indispensable knowledge and goals of their own.

### `adaptable`

- The player may be an unnamed or composite forward scout rather than a literal
  reenactment of Malone, provided the role does not absorb every companion's
  mastery.
- Travel time, plateau scale, number of companions, fauna density and evidence
  inventory may be compressed for the bounded prototype.
- A firearm may represent the expedition's limited defense without reproducing
  the novel's battles, body counts or hunting trophies.
- Exact species names may be replaced by field labels until observation is
  sufficient; the source itself contains uncertainty.

### `open`

- The source does not define ammunition counts, camera capacity, precise threat
  detection radii, stealth meters, sprint stamina, fail checkpoints or scoring.
- It does not state which single proof item a solo scout would prioritize, how
  a five-to-eight-minute route is arranged, or how much map detail is visible
  before observation.
- It does not provide a modern scientific reconstruction, so anatomy, gait,
  color and vocalization require original art direction and documented
  reference choices.

### `conflicted`

- Malone's estimated thirty-by-twenty-mile plateau and ten-mile-circumference
  lake are a single visual estimate, not surveyed measurements (Chapter 11).
- Challenger and Summerlee disagree on several classifications, and some fauna
  remain unnamed (Chapters 10–11, 15–16).
- The expedition sometimes treats photographs and specimens as decisive while
  the London audience finds them forgeable or non-exclusive (Chapters 4, 16).
- The downloaded catalog/header update metadata differ as recorded in
  `../source/SOURCE.md`; the exact local hash, not an inferred edition history,
  is the adaptation source.

## Cultural and ethical context

The novel repeatedly uses colonial hierarchy, racist labels, evolutionary
ranking of living peoples, paternalism, conquest language and dehumanizing
descriptions. It also frames hunting, resource extraction and the destruction
or enslavement of a population as adventure or progress. Chapters 6, 13, 14,
15 and 16 are especially important evidence of that lens.

The prototype must not:

- turn ethnicity or human-like anatomy into a hostile-creature readability
  shortcut;
- reproduce the Chapter 14 extermination as a victory lap, score source or
  promotional screenshot;
- erase Zambo's or Maretas's work by assigning their logistics and local
  knowledge to the white expedition;
- use pseudo-scientific racial hierarchy as true lore;
- reward trophy hunting or diamonds as the core success condition; or
- borrow imagery, creature designs or audio from modern adaptations.

This boundary does not hide the source. It records the harmful framing so that
later concept, world and art owners cannot accidentally normalize it. The
prototype's compatible source focus is ecological observation, fragile proof,
route-finding, limited defense and voluntary extraction.

## Risks and design gaps for the next gate

1. **Proof readability:** determine how a player can distinguish sighting,
   recorded evidence, retained evidence and extracted proof without a text-heavy
   journal.
2. **Threat legibility:** select at most the threat classes needed to show
   materially different perception/counterplay within one short run.
3. **Spatial compression:** preserve the edge-camp → forest/water → objective →
   exit logic without pretending to simulate a thirty-mile open world.
4. **Defensive action:** prove that the firearm creates escape space and cost,
   not an optimal creature-clearing loop.
5. **Local knowledge:** if the cave chart or Accala route is used, make its
   authorship visible and avoid treating the inhabitants as quest dispensers.
6. **Scientific framing:** establish an original visual research method that is
   recognizably prehistoric without claiming the 1912 taxonomy is current.
7. **Ending credibility:** define a small set of proof outcomes the player can
   understand from play—lost, weak, credible—without claiming that subjective
   audience belief is deterministically verified.

These are questions for concept selection. This source bible does not choose a
level layout, final creature design, combat model or ending implementation.
